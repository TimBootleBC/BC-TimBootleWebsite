Food

How to stock a 3-day and 2-week emergency food supply for a household of 4 to 6, and how to keep food safe when the power is out in the Charleston area.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

You can go longer without food than without water, but a household under stress still needs familiar, easy calories — especially children. The aim is not a novel “survival menu.” It is food you already eat, that does not need a working refrigerator or, if possible, a stove.

How much to keep

Ready.gov asks households to store at least a several-day supply of non-perishable food. The American Red Cross is more specific: 3 days for an evacuation bag, and about 2 weeks at home.

A practical calorie planning figure used by many preparedness guides is about 1,500 calories per person per day for short-term emergency eating (more for teens or very active adults, less for some small children — follow medical or pediatric advice for infants). For a household of 4 to 6:

Period Rough calorie total What it looks like
3 days About 18,000–27,000 calories Ready-to-eat meals and snacks in the go-bags, plus a manual can opener if anything is canned.
14 days About 84,000–126,000 calories A deep pantry of foods you normally buy, rotated first-in, first-out.

Include infant formula or other special diets, and pet food, on top of these numbers. Ready-to-feed formula is simpler than mixing powder if water quality is uncertain.

Choose foods your family will actually eat. A stressful week is a poor time to introduce a new diet. Remember salt, any needed condiments, and a bit of comfort food for children.

What belongs in the pantry

Useful staples, drawn from official kit lists:

  • Ready-to-eat canned meats, beans, fruits, and vegetables
  • Canned or boxed juices and shelf-stable milk
  • Peanut butter, nut butters, or similar proteins
  • Dried fruit, nuts, granola, cereal, and protein or granola bars
  • Crackers, and other dry staples in sealed containers
  • Ready-to-eat meals or pouch foods that need only hot water, if you have a safe way to boil water
  • Infant and toddler foods if needed
  • A manual can opener, disposable or washable plates and utensils, and paper towels

Store canned goods in a cool, dry place. Keep opened dry goods in closed containers. Check dates when you check the rest of the kit, and eat what is nearing its date as part of ordinary meals.

The simplest way to build a two-week supply is to buy a little extra of what you already use and rotate it: finish the older packages first, and restock. Specialty long-shelf-life food is optional, not required, if your ordinary pantry is deep enough and you have a way to prepare it safely.

Cooking when the power is out

Commercially canned food can be eaten cold from a clean can. If you heat a can, take the label off, wash the can, and open it before heating.

Use charcoal grills, camp stoves, and other fuel appliances outdoors only, well away from windows and doors. Never use a gas oven or stovetop to heat the house. See Power and lighting for carbon monoxide rules, which also apply to cooking fuel.

If you cannot cook, lean on foods that need no heat at all.

Food safety without refrigeration

Bacteria grow quickly in food between 40°F and 140°F. Ready.gov and food-safety agencies use these rules of thumb:

  • Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible.
  • An unopened refrigerator typically keeps food cold for about 4 hours.
  • A full freezer stays cold for about 48 hours; a half-full freezer for about 24 hours.
  • Use a refrigerator or freezer thermometer. Refrigerated or frozen food should stay at 40°F or below.
  • Thawed food that still contains ice crystals, or feels as cold as if it were refrigerated, can usually be cooked or refrozen. When in doubt, throw it out.
  • Discard meat, poultry, fish, eggs, leftovers, and other perishables that have been above 40°F for 2 hours or more.
  • After the outage, throw away food with an odd smell, color, or texture. Do not taste it to “check.”
  • Discard any food that has touched floodwater, and any cans that are swollen, badly dented, or rusted through.

If you expect a long outage, a cooler with ice can save a smaller amount of high-value food. Dry ice is sometimes used for freezers (a common figure is about 25 pounds for a 10-cubic-foot freezer for 3 to 4 days), but it must not touch food directly and must be handled with heavy dry gloves in a ventilated area.

If medicines that need refrigeration have been warm for more than a day, ask a pharmacist or doctor before using them, unless the label clearly allows room-temperature storage.
