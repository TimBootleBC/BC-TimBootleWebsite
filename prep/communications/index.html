Communications

How a Charleston-area household can receive official alerts, stay in touch if cell networks are congested, and keep a paper backup when phones and the internet are down.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

In an emergency, the most important messages are official ones: evacuate, stay home, a tornado warning, a boil-water notice, a road closure. Your plan should assume that cell service will be slow or down, that the home internet will die with the power, and that a phone with a dead battery is just a dark piece of glass.

How official alerts reach you

Use more than one channel. They overlap on purpose.

Wireless Emergency Alerts (WEA) are short messages from authorized agencies to compatible phones in an affected area. They can sound a loud tone even if the phone is on silent. You do not sign up. If you are driving, stop in a safe, legal place before you read the message. Do what the alert says.

The Emergency Alert System (EAS) breaks into local radio and television with the same class of official warnings.

NOAA Weather Radio is a dedicated radio service from the National Weather Service. A tone-alert weather radio, on batteries or a crank, will keep working when the TV and Wi-Fi do not. Program it for the Charleston County area and test it. NWS Charleston is the forecast office for this region: weather.gov/chs.

County mass notification (CHSAlerts in Charleston County). Counties send opt-in alerts by text, phone call, or email for evacuations, severe weather, and other local emergencies. Sign up on the official county site so the system has your address and the ways you want to be reached. Berkeley, Dorchester, and other nearby counties have their own systems — sign up where you live, and consider signing up for a workplace or second-home address if you spend a lot of time there.

SCEMD offers the official South Carolina Emergency Manager app, with zone maps, a personal plan, and state alerts. The state hurricane hub is hurricane.sc.

The FEMA app can deliver National Weather Service alerts for up to five U.S. locations, which is useful if family members are in different cities.

Local radio stations remain important after a storm, which is why a simple AM/FM radio still belongs in the house kit.

A household communication plan

Ready.gov’s planning questions are the right ones: How will you receive warnings? How will you contact one another if you are apart? Where will you meet if you cannot go home?

Write down, on paper:

  • A primary out-of-area contact (someone in another city who can take check-in messages if local lines are jammed)
  • A local meeting place near home, and a second meeting place outside the neighborhood
  • School and workplace pickup rules, including who is authorized to collect children
  • Phone numbers for family, neighbors, doctors, insurance, the veterinarian, and utilities
  • Social media or message-app accounts you might use — and a reminder that official sources come first, rumors do not

Text messages and social-media check-ins often get through when a voice call cannot. After a disaster, keep voice calls short and save them for true emergencies so networks stay usable for 911.

If the network is down, go to the meeting place at the time you agreed, rather than waiting indefinitely for a call that cannot be placed.

Agree who will check on whom — a next-door neighbor who lives alone, a relative across town — and how that check happens if phones are down (a knock, a note, a prearranged window).

Power for communications

Keep power banks charged, with a cable for every phone in the household. See Power and lighting. A car charger helps if you can use the vehicle safely and have fuel, but a running car in a closed garage is a carbon monoxide hazard.

Landline phones that need house power (most modern ones) will not work in an outage. Do not assume a “home phone” is a backup unless you have confirmed how it is powered.

When card payments and the internet are down

Have some cash in small bills, because card terminals and ATMs may be offline. Keep a paper map of the area; street signs can be down and phones may not load maps. Copies of key documents belong in a waterproof pouch — see Documents and money.
