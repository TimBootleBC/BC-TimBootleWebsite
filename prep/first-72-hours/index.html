Preparedness

The first 72 hours

Why households are asked to be ready for the first three days of an emergency, what to have at home versus in a go-bag, and planning quantities for a Charleston-area family of 4 to 6.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

Emergency managers talk about the first 72 hours because that is often how long it can take for help to reach every neighborhood after a widespread disaster. Roads may be blocked, 911 centers and hospitals will be busy with life-threatening calls, and grocery stores, pharmacies, and gas stations may be closed or unable to take cards if the power is out.

Seventy-two hours is a minimum. The American Red Cross recommends a 3-day supply in a bag you can take if you evacuate, and about two weeks of water and food at home. After hurricanes on the Atlantic and Gulf coasts, some households have gone much longer than three days without reliable power or water. If you can do both — a packed bag and a two-week home cupboard — you have covered the usual stay-or-go problem without having to predict which one you will face.

Six needs, plus water and food

A useful way to think about a ready household is by need, not by a pile of gadgets:

Need What it means in practice
Information A way to hear official updates if the phone network, internet, or power is down — typically a battery or hand-crank radio that can receive NOAA Weather Radio.
Power A way to keep phones and essential devices on: charged power banks, spare batteries, and, if you have one, a generator used only outdoors.
Light A flashlight or headlamp for each person, plus spare batteries. Headlamps leave both hands free. Electric lights are safer than candles.
Medical A first-aid kit you actually know how to use, plus several days (ideally a week) of prescription medicines, spare glasses, and device batteries.
Climate A way to stay cooler in Charleston heat without air conditioning, and warm enough if a winter outage knocks out the heat. Blankets, airflow, and shade matter more than novelty gear.
Protection Work gloves, a whistle, rain gear, sturdy shoes, and simple respiratory protection for dusty or moldy cleanup. Plastic sheeting and duct tape if you are told to shelter in place.

Water, food, sanitation, documents, cash, and pets sit beside those six needs. They are easy to overlook if you only think about a “kit.” They are also the items that run out first.

At home versus in a bag

Keep a home stock in a cool, dry place everyone knows — a hall closet, a sturdy bin, or a clearly marked corner of a garage that does not flood first. Store water next to it. The home stock is for sheltering in place: extra water and food, hygiene supplies, tools, a fire extinguisher, and comfort items for children.

Keep a go-bag (one bag per adult who can carry one, and a smaller bag or identified items for children) packed and close to an exit. The bag is for when you are told to leave, or when you choose to leave early. It should have three days of the essentials, medicines, documents, chargers, a change of clothes, and whatever children or pets need so you are not choosing items in the hallway. See Evacuation and go-bag.

If you also spend time at work or in the car, a smaller 24-hour kit there covers the chance that you are not at home when something starts.

Planning quantities for 4 to 6 people

These figures use official U.S. planning rules (1 gallon of water per person per day; several days of food at a minimum) and the Red Cross two-week home target. In extreme heat, water needs can rise, so treat the gallon as a floor, not a ceiling.

Three days

  • Water: 12 gallons for 4 people, 18 gallons for 6, plus pet water.
  • Food: meals and snacks your household will eat with no cooking, or with only a safe outdoor method. Include a manual can opener.
  • Medicines: at least three days, and a week if prescriptions allow.
  • Light: one flashlight or headlamp per person, spare batteries.
  • Power: at least one fully charged power bank and cables that fit every phone in the house.
  • Information: NOAA weather radio, plus important numbers on paper.

Two weeks at home (build this after the three-day layer is in place)

  • Water: 56 gallons for 4 people, 84 gallons for 6 — a mix of store-bought bottles and food-grade containers. That is also about 15 gallons per person, a common independent planning figure for two weeks.
  • Food: roughly 1,500 or more calories per person per day of shelf-stable food, adjusted for ages and medical diets. For 4 to 6 people that is on the order of 84,000–126,000 calories, which looks like a deep ordinary pantry, not a specialty purchase, if you rotate what you already cook.
  • Hygiene and sanitation supplies for the same period, including bags, wipes, and unscented bleach.
  • Fuel for any generator, stored legally and safely, with a realistic run-time plan — fuel often runs out before the outage does.

Details and safety notes are on the water, food, power, and sanitation pages.

What “ready” looks like on an ordinary week

  • Everyone at home can point to the supplies and the bags.
  • Phones are signed up for county alerts, and a NOAA radio is programmed and tested.
  • You know your evacuation zone by address and have a paper map and a meeting place.
  • Prescriptions are not left on a “we will refill tomorrow” margin.
  • You have walked through the family plan once, including how to shut off utilities only if officials tell you to or if you know it is safe.

A kit you never practice with is only a box. Checking expiration dates twice a year — a common cue is when you change smoke-alarm batteries — keeps the plan honest.
