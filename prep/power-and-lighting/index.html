Power and lighting

How a Charleston-area household of 4 to 6 can keep lights, phones, and essential devices working during a power outage, and how to use a generator without carbon monoxide risk.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

In the Lowcountry, a hurricane or severe thunderstorm can leave neighborhoods without electricity for hours or, after a major storm, for many days. An outage can also shut down well pumps, some types of medical equipment, card readers at stores and gas stations, and the internet. Plan first for light and phone charging. Add larger power only if you can use it safely.

Light

  • Give each person a flashlight or, better, a headlamp, and keep one in the go-bag and one by the bed.
  • Store spare batteries in the sizes those lights actually take. Check them when you inspect the kit.
  • Chemical light sticks need no batteries and make no flame. They are useful for hallways, children’s rooms, and marking a path, not as a main light.
  • Electric lights are safer than candles or oil lamps. If you must use an open flame, never leave it unattended, keep it far from curtains, and have a fire extinguisher and a way out.

Practice finding the lights in the dark once, so nobody is searching a drawer during an outage.

Phones and small devices

A charged power bank and the correct cables will keep phones on for official alerts, maps, and a few essential calls. For a household of 4 to 6, one large power bank is a start; two or more is more realistic if several people need a phone at once. Recharge the banks as part of ordinary life so they are not empty when the power fails.

If someone depends on a powered medical device or on refrigerated medicine, talk to their clinician before storm season about backup power and how long the medicine can stay warm. That plan belongs in the family plan and should not depend on a last-minute store trip.

Unplug or switch off sensitive electronics and appliances such as TVs, computers, and window air conditioners when the power goes out, so a surge does not damage them when it returns. Leave one lamp switched on so you know when service is back.

Radios and medical backups

A battery or hand-crank radio that can receive NOAA Weather Radio is the most reliable way to hear watches, warnings, and post-storm updates if cell service is congested or down. See Communications.

Keep spare batteries for the radio and for any medical device that takes them.

During an outage

  • Report the outage to your electric utility (in much of the Charleston area that is Dominion Energy South Carolina — confirm the current app, text, or phone number on the utility’s site; a widely published reporting number is 888-333-4465) and to any backup you use, such as a county alert feed.
  • Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. Food timing is on the food page.
  • Check on neighbors who may need help, especially anyone who uses medical equipment or who may struggle in the heat.
  • If the house becomes dangerously hot or cold and a community cooling or warming center is open, consider going there. Local officials and the Charleston resources page explain how those announcements usually appear.

Generators and carbon monoxide

A portable generator can run a refrigerator, some lights, and medical equipment. It can also kill people in minutes if it is used indoors or too close to the house. Carbon monoxide (CO) is colorless and odorless.

Ready.gov’s safety rules are strict for a reason:

  • Operate generators outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, and attached garages.
  • Never run a generator in a house, garage, basement, crawl space, or shed, even with the door open.
  • Keep the unit dry and out of floodwater. A wet generator, or wet cords, can cause shock.
  • Use heavy-duty outdoor-rated extension cords, fully unwound. Do not back-feed a household panel with a homemade hookup; that can electrify utility lines and endanger line workers. A licensed electrician can install a legal transfer switch if you want whole-panel backup.
  • Let the engine cool before refueling. Fuel on a hot engine can ignite.
  • Store fuel in approved containers, away from living spaces and ignition sources, and in the amounts and locations local fire rules allow.
  • Install carbon monoxide alarms with battery backup on every level of the home, and test them regularly.

The same outdoor-only rule applies to camp stoves, charcoal grills, and gasoline tools. Never use a gas oven or stovetop to heat the house.

If a CO alarm sounds, get everyone (including pets) into fresh air and call 911. Do not go back inside until emergency responders say it is safe.

Building up capacity over time

A practical order of investment is: good lights and batteries; power banks kept charged; a NOAA radio; CO and smoke alarms; then, if you choose, a generator sized to a short, written list of loads (refrigerator, medical device, a few lights) rather than to the whole house. Solar chargers and larger battery stations can help with phones and fans, especially after the fuel for a generator is gone, but they are a supplement, not a requirement, for a basic 72-hour plan.
