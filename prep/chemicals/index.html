Chemicals and water treatment safety

Last reviewed: 11 November 2026

Safe bleach disinfection of water that is only contaminated by germs, basic chemical storage and handling, and the never-mix pairs that produce toxic gas, for households in Charleston, Dorchester, and Berkeley County.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

This is a short reference for the household chemicals that are most useful (and most often misused) during a power or water outage. It covers safe disinfection of water that is only biologically contaminated, basic storage and handling rules, and the never-mix pairs that produce toxic gas. It does not cover industrial chemical releases, fuel or chemical contamination of water, or advanced DIY chemistry.

Always follow the instructions in an official alert or from local officials, even if they differ from the guidance here. If life is at risk, call 911.

Water disinfection for germs only

Boiling or chemical disinfection kills bacteria and viruses. It does not remove fuel, toxic chemicals, heavy metals, or radioactive materials. If you suspect chemical contamination, use stored bottled water and contact your local health department or utility. Do not try to treat it.

For water you believe is only biologically contaminated (and after filtering if it is cloudy):

Unscented household bleach (sodium hypochlorite, 5–8.25%)

Use only plain, unscented bleach with no added cleaners, fragrances, or color-safe additives. Check the label for the exact strength.

Bleach strength Drops per gallon (clear water) Notes
6% 8 drops Double the amount if the water is cloudy or colored
8.25% 6 drops Same rule for cloudy water

Stir, wait 30 minutes, then check for a slight chlorine odor. If there is none, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. Pour the water between two clean containers to reduce the taste if needed. Source: CDC and EPA.

Calcium hypochlorite (pool shock)

Dry calcium hypochlorite (typically 65–70% available chlorine) has a much longer shelf life than liquid bleach when kept dry and sealed. It is a concentrated oxidizer. Follow the manufacturer’s directions or a reliable official dosing chart for drinking-water use; do not guess. Store it in its original container, cool, dry, and away from acids, ammonia, and organic materials. See the never-mix list below.

Never-mix rules (memorize these)

These combinations produce toxic gas, fire, or violent reactions. Keep the items on separate shelves and never put them in the same bucket, drain, or spray bottle.

Item Never mix with Result
Bleach or calcium hypochlorite Acids (vinegar, muriatic acid, toilet bowl cleaner) Chlorine gas
Bleach or calcium hypochlorite Ammonia or ammonia-based cleaners Chloramine gas
Bleach or calcium hypochlorite Oils, glycerin, or other organics Fire or explosion risk
Lye (drain cleaner) Aluminum (foil, pots, some containers) Flammable hydrogen gas
Lye Acids Violent heat and gas

Additional practical rules:

  • Never store chemicals in food or drink containers.
  • Keep original labels; if a container is damaged, replace the label or the container.
  • Store away from heat, sunlight, children, and pets. Lock or childproof cabinets if you have young children.
  • Do not smoke or use open flames near flammable liquids or aerosols.
  • When cleaning spills, ventilate the area and use the product’s own instructions.

Storage notes that matter for preparedness

  • Liquid bleach loses strength over time (often months, not years). Rotate it. Calcium hypochlorite lasts longer when kept dry.
  • Do not store large quantities of concentrated oxidizers or acids if you do not have a clear, labeled plan for using and rotating them.
  • In a flood or hurricane, move household chemicals to higher ground if it is still safe to do so (same guidance as on the evacuation page).
  • After an event, follow local instructions for household hazardous waste disposal. Do not dump chemicals into storm drains or ditches.

What this page does not cover

  • Treatment of water known or suspected to contain fuel, industrial chemicals, or radioactive material.
  • Stocking or using lye, strong acids, or other compounds for soap-making, food preservation, or other advanced uses.
  • Response to a major chemical release from a facility or transportation accident. For those, follow local emergency alerts and the guidance of the Charleston County, Berkeley County, or Dorchester County Local Emergency Planning Committee (LEPC).
