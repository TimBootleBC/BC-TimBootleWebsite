Family plan and checklists

A printable family emergency plan and checklists for a Charleston-area household of 4 to 6, covering alerts, meeting places, fire escape, shelter-in-place, and a twice-yearly supply review.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

A plan is a short written agreement the household can follow when people are tired, scared, or in different places. Ready.gov’s core questions are enough: How will you get alerts? Where will you shelter? How will you leave, and by which route? How will you contact one another? Is the kit up to date?

Fill this in on paper. Keep one copy in the document pouch, one on the refrigerator, and a photo of it in your phones. Practice it once a year, including a fire-exit walk-through with children.

This is a template, not an official form. For a fillable federal version, use Ready.gov’s family emergency plan. The official South Carolina Emergency Manager app can also store a personal plan and your zone.

Fire: get out, stay out, call 911

Work out two ways out of every sleeping room. Keep keys to security bars or window locks where everyone can find them. Close doors behind you as you leave if you can, to slow smoke. Meet at the local meeting place. Do not go back inside.

If the way out is blocked, get everyone into a room, close the door, seal the bottom with a towel, open a window, call 911, and signal for help. If clothing catches fire: stop, drop, and roll.

Test smoke alarms monthly. Put a heat alarm in the kitchen if smoke alarms there false-trigger, and put carbon monoxide alarms on every level, especially near fuel-burning appliances. Do not leave cooking unattended. Do not charge phones or power banks under pillows or on bedding.

If you are told to shelter in place

“Shelter in place” is not the same as an ordinary stay-at-home day. If officials say the air outside may be unsafe, or you see a plume or heavy debris in the air:

  • Bring people and pets inside.
  • Close and lock windows and doors. Close fireplace dampers.
  • Turn off fans, air conditioning, and forced-air heating if you can do so safely.
  • Go to an interior room with few windows. Take the emergency kit if it is not contaminated.
  • If officials tell you to seal the room, use pre-cut plastic sheeting and duct tape over windows, doors, and vents. Cut the plastic several inches larger than each opening and label the sheets in advance.

Listen to official radio or alerts until you are told it is safe to come out. This is a short-term measure.

For a tornado or very high wind, an interior room on the lowest floor, away from windows, is the usual guidance. For a flood, go up — but not into a closed attic.

Twice-yearly kit review

A common cue is the weekend you change smoke-alarm batteries or when clocks change.

  • Water: dates, quantity still enough for 4–6 people (12–18 gallons for 3 days; 56–84 gallons for 2 weeks), pet water included
  • Food: dates, calories and special diets, manual can opener
  • Medicines: at least a 7-day supply if possible; batteries for devices; glasses
  • Lights, radio, power banks charged, spare batteries
  • First-aid kit restocked
  • Documents packet updated; some cash still in the pouch
  • Go-bags complete; children’s clothes still fit; seasonal clothes swapped
  • Pet kits, carriers, and microchip records
  • Shutters or plywood hardware still together; fuel for a generator still fresh and legally stored
  • Alert sign-ups still work; new phones added to WEA-capable accounts
  • Walk through the fire exits and the evacuation packing list once

If you witness a public-safety emergency

Call 911. Follow instructions from law enforcement or from an official alert. If you are in a crowded place and an attack is underway, the federal “Run, Hide, Fight” public guidance is the U.S. reference; use official sources rather than rumors, and keep away from the scene once you are out. This site is not a tactical guide.
