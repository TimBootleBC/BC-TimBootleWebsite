Water

How much emergency water a household of 4 to 6 should store in the Charleston area, how to store it safely, and how to treat water if the tap supply is cut off or contaminated.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

After a hurricane, flood, or long outage, tap water may stop, lose pressure, or become unsafe. Stored drinking water is the single most useful household supply you can have, and it is also the item people most often under-buy. Do not count on having time to fill a bathtub or reach a store once a warning is issued.

How much to store

Ready.gov and the American Red Cross both use 1 gallon per person per day for drinking and sanitation. A typically active adult may drink about three-quarters of a gallon, but the rest of that gallon covers hygiene, cooking, and a margin for heat. Children, nursing parents, and people who are ill often need more. In very hot weather, needs can rise sharply — sometimes doubling — which matters in Charleston summers, especially if the power and air conditioning are out.

For a household of 4 to 6 people:

Period 4 people 6 people Notes
3 days 12 gallons 18 gallons The evacuation / first-72-hours layer. Keep some of this in the go-bag as sealed bottles.
14 days 56 gallons 84 gallons A solid home target (about 15 gallons per person).

Add a similar daily allowance for each pet. If someone in the house has a medical need that uses extra water, add that too.

Never ration drinking water on your own unless authorities tell you to. Drink what you need today and look for a safe source for tomorrow. Reduce how much you need by staying in the shade, limiting hard work in the heat, and avoiding alcohol and heavy caffeine, which can dehydrate you.

How to store it

Best option: commercially bottled water, left in its original sealed containers, in a cool, dark place. Keep it off concrete if you can, and do not store it next to gasoline, pesticides, or household chemicals. Check dates and rotate.

If you fill your own containers: use food-grade water barrels, jugs, or stacks designed for drinking water. Wash them with dish soap, rinse, then sanitize with 1 teaspoon of unscented household chlorine bleach in a quart of water, and rinse again before filling with chlorinated tap water. Do not use empty milk jugs; the plastic degrades and is hard to clean. Soft-drink bottles in good condition are a short-term alternative some local guides still mention, but dedicated food-grade containers are better for a two-week supply.

Replace water you bottled yourself about every six months. Label containers with the date.

A water heater can hold many gallons of potentially usable water, but only if you know how to isolate it and drain it safely, and only after you have shut off power or gas to the heater so it cannot run dry and be damaged. Learn that procedure now, from the heater manual or a plumber, not during a flood.

If you run out of stored water

Treat any water of uncertain quality before you drink it, brush teeth with it, make ice, or wash food or dishes. Let sediment settle, or pour the water through a clean cloth or coffee filter first.

Ready.gov describes three household methods:

Boiling is the most reliable way to kill disease-causing organisms. Bring the water to a rolling boil for one full minute, then let it cool. Pouring it between two clean containers after it cools can improve the taste.

Chlorination with regular unscented liquid household bleach that contains 5.25–6.0% sodium hypochlorite can also kill many microbes. Do not use scented, color-safe, or “splash-less” bleach, or bleach with added cleaners. The usual Ready.gov dose is 1/8 teaspoon (about 16 drops) of bleach per gallon of clear water. Stir, and wait 30 minutes. The water should have a slight chlorine smell. If it does not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes. If there is still no chlorine odor, do not drink it; find another source. Cloudy water needs to be filtered or settled first, and may need a doubled dose — when in doubt, prefer boiling.

Distillation (boiling water and collecting only the condensed steam) can remove some microbes that resist boiling or bleach, as well as many salts, heavy metals, and other chemicals. It is slow and is a last-resort method at home, not a first-line plan.

Portable filters designed for hiking can be a useful complement, especially in a go-bag, but you must use them as the manufacturer directs and understand what they do and do not remove. Filters are not a reason to skip stored water.

Do not drink or cook with floodwater. Per CDC, boiling or disinfecting will not make water that may contain fuel, chemicals, or other toxic contaminants safe. Get water from another source: your stored supply, bottled water, or an official water distribution point.

Using water carefully

  • Drink known-safe stored water first.
  • Keep a share for sanitation: handwashing, cleaning wounds, and a basic toilet setup if the sewer is out. See Sanitation and hygiene.
  • If officials issue a boil-water advisory, follow it even if the tap looks clear.
  • After a flood, household pipes and filters may themselves be contaminated. Wait for the utility’s guidance before drinking from the tap again.
