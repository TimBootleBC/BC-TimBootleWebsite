Sanitation and hygiene

How to manage toilets, trash, and personal hygiene for a household of 4 to 6 when running water or sewer service is disrupted after a storm or outage.

This is general household information, not official emergency instructions. In an emergency, follow local officials, the National Weather Service, and call 911 if life is at risk.

A house can become unsafe quickly if toilets cannot be flushed, trash piles up, or people cannot wash their hands. After floods and long outages, stomach illness and infected wounds are common problems, and they are mostly preventable with boring supplies: bags, wipes, soap, and unscented bleach.

If the water is off but the sewer still works

You can often flush a toilet by pouring a bucket of water (not your drinking reserve, if you can help it — greywater from a water heater drain or collected rain in a clean bucket may be enough) into the bowl. Do not flush if officials have said the sewer is backed up or if the house is flooding.

Wash hands with stored water and soap when you can. Alcohol-based hand sanitizer is a backup when water is scarce, but soap and water are better after using the toilet or handling food, and sanitizer does not work well on visibly dirty hands.

If the toilet will not work

Set up a temporary toilet before you need one, not after.

A sturdy 5-gallon bucket with a tight lid, heavy-duty bags, and a way to sit safely is the usual household method. Line the bucket, use it as a toilet, add a small amount of unscented household disinfectant or a thin layer of cat litter if that is the system you have chosen, then seal the bag and store it in a second lidded container away from food and living areas until collection is possible. Follow local public-health directions if they issue any after a disaster — they take priority.

Keep a dedicated supply, sized for 4 to 6 people for at least three days and preferably two weeks:

  • Toilet paper and moist wipes (store wipes so they do not dry out)
  • Extra heavy-duty garbage bags and twist ties
  • A bucket with a tight lid, and a second container for sealed waste
  • Soap, hand sanitizer, and paper towels
  • Unscented household bleach for disinfecting surfaces (this is the same type used for water treatment; keep it clearly labeled and away from children)
  • Menstrual supplies, diapers, and incontinence supplies well beyond a three-day estimate — these run out faster than people expect
  • Personal toiletries: toothbrushes, toothpaste, deodorant, and any other daily items

Ready.gov also lists moist towelettes, garbage bags, and plastic ties as part of a basic kit for exactly this reason.

Cleaning and infection control

  • Disinfect kitchen and bathroom surfaces with a bleach solution or a household disinfectant, following the product label. A commonly used dilute bleach cleaner for surfaces (not for drinking water) is about 1 part bleach to 9 or 10 parts water, mixed fresh. Do not mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners.
  • Keep food preparation areas separate from any emergency toilet.
  • Take trash out of the living space daily if you can. Stored garbage attracts pests and is a fire and health problem in a closed house.
  • After a flood, assume silt and standing water are contaminated. Wear gloves and boots, and see official cleanup guidance before you try to dry out the house. Do not use flood-soaked sponges or toothbrushes.

Insect repellent and sunscreen belong with hygiene supplies in this climate. Mosquitoes can surge after standing water, and cleanup work happens in full sun.

Hygiene in a go-bag

Each person’s bag should have a small soap or sanitizer, wipes, a toothbrush, any menstrual or incontinence items, and a compact towel. These are often forgotten when people pack only food and chargers.
